How to read the credentials

  • full PhD / PsyD (licensed psychologist): doctoral training, usually the deepest grounding in CBT, exposure and DBT, and able to do formal assessment. LMHC, LICSW and LMFT are full master's-level licenses that allow independent practice.
  • associate LMHCA / LMFTA / LICSWA / LSWAIC, or a PT "Prelicense": master's-level clinicians practising under a named supervisor while they accrue hours. Psychology Today profiles often print the supervisor.
  • Linehan Board certified (DBT-LBC): an independent certification for DBT clinicians and programs. It's the best signal that "DBT" means the full model rather than a few skills.
  • IOCDF BTTI: the International OCD Foundation's Behavior Therapy Training Institute, a specialist ERP course.
  • PSYPACT: the psychologist can provide telehealth across participating states.

At 12, keep in mind

  • Many "teen" DBT programs start at 13. EBTCS adolescent DBT is "for individuals ages 13-18" (EBTCS), the UW DBT Clinic serves "adults and adolescents (13+)" (UW), and Ryther's DBT IOP page now says "teens ages 14–17" (Ryther). For a 12-year-old, look for "child and adolescent" practices instead, such as Cadence, the EBTCS Child Anxiety Center or Seattle Children's.
  • Parents carry more of the treatment at this age. Look for clinicians who name parent work explicitly: SPACE (a parent-led anxiety program from the Yale Child Study Center, offered by Karen Pang and EBTCS), behavioural parent training (EBTCS, Marisa Keller), family sessions (Sanaz Amini, Larisa Whipple), and Seattle Children's parent-and-child groups. Ask every candidate how often a parent is in the room and what parents practise at home.
  • Washington's age of consent for outpatient mental health is 13. RCW 71.34.530: "Any adolescent may request and receive outpatient treatment without the consent of the adolescent's parent. Parental authorization … is required for outpatient treatment of a minor under the age of thirteen." RCW 71.34.020(3) defines "adolescent" as "a minor thirteen years of age or older" (RCW 71.34.530, RCW 71.34.020). So at 12 the parents consent to therapy and, as the child's personal representatives under HIPAA (45 CFR 164.502(g)), generally have access to the records. From 13 the child can start outpatient therapy on their own consent, and confidentiality shifts toward the teen: under RCW 70.02.265 a clinician "shall not proactively" release a 13+ adolescent's mental-health information to a parent unless the teen wants it or there's an imminent safety threat, must tell the teen when a disclosure is made, and must document a refusal of a parent's request. The state referral line likewise won't share a 13+ caller's information with parents without consent. Parents can still bring a 13–17-year-old for evaluation and inpatient treatment without the teen's consent (RCW 71.34.600), and clinicians are "encouraged" to share more with parents in that parent-initiated treatment.
  • Ask about the 13th birthday up front. If therapy will run past it, ask the clinician how they'll handle the change in consent and what parents will still hear.

Questions to ask at the intro call

  1. Age fit: How many of your current clients are 11–13? Do you treat this age individually, or mainly through parents?
  2. Supervisor (associates and trainees): Who supervises you, and do they treat children and teens?
  3. Methods: For anxiety, do you do exposure (ERP) with between-session practice? For mood or emotion swings, do you deliver DBT or CBT protocols, or an eclectic mix?
  4. Measuring progress: Which standard measures do you track (PHQ-A, GAD-7, RCADS, etc.), how often, and when would you change plan or refer?
  5. Parent involvement: Are there parent sessions or parent coaching (SPACE, behavioural parent training)? What stays confidential now, what changes at 13, and how do you handle safety?
  6. Aetna and cost: Are you in-network with our specific insurance plan (Aetna or otherwise)? If not, do you provide superbills, and what is the fee per session?
